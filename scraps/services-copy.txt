Probate Valuation
Probate Property Valuations in Dublin and Leinster
Accurate, well-evidenced date-of-death valuations for probate and estate administration — prepared for solicitors, executors and personal representatives.
What is a probate valuation?
A probate valuation establishes the open market value of a property as at the date of the owner's death. That figure is required for the Statement of Affairs (Probate) Form SA.2 submitted to Revenue, and it forms the basis on which the estate is administered and any Capital Acquisitions Tax is calculated.
The date matters more than people expect. The valuation is not what the house is worth today — it is what it was worth on a specific past date, which may be months or even years ago. Getting that right requires evidence from the market as it stood then, not as it stands now.
Why the figure needs to be defensible
A probate valuation is submitted to Revenue and relied upon by beneficiaries. Under-value the property and the estate risks a Revenue query, an amended return and, potentially, interest and penalties. Over-value it and beneficiaries may pay tax on value that was never there. Where the property is later sold, the difference between the probate value and the sale price has capital gains consequences for the estate.
A figure that is simply asserted is difficult to defend if it is questioned. A figure supported by documented comparable evidence from around the valuation date is straightforward to defend, because the reasoning is on the page.

What is an LPT valuation?
Local Property Tax is a self-assessed tax. Revenue does not tell you what your property is worth; you declare a valuation band, and you are responsible for that declaration being reasonable and defensible.
For most homes, a careful look at recent local sales makes the band obvious. For a good many others it does not — and those are the cases we are instructed on.
When homeowners get a professional LPT valuation
The property sits close to a band boundary, where the difference between two bands is real money each year
The property is unusual, and there are few or no genuine comparables — a period home, a converted building, a large site, or a house in an area that rarely trades
Revenue has queried the declared band and evidence is needed to support it
The property is held in an estate, a trust or a company, where the declaration needs to be supported by documentation
The owner is an executor or attorney declaring on someone else's behalf and wants the decision properly evidenced
Self-assessment means you carry the risk
Because LPT is self-assessed, an under-declared band is your exposure, not Revenue's error. If the declaration is queried, what protects you is being able to show that the band was arrived at reasonably, on the basis of evidence available at the valuation date.
That is precisely what a written valuation from an accredited valuer provides — a documented, dated record of the comparable evidence and the reasoning behind the band you declared.

Blue Book Valuation
Blue Book Valuations — European Valuation Standards
Valuation reports prepared to European Valuation Standards by a TEGoVA-accredited valuer — recognised by lenders and institutions across Ireland and Europe.
What is a Blue Book valuation?
The Blue Book is the property industry's name for European Valuation Standards, published by TEGoVA, the European Group of Valuers' Associations. EVS sets out the definitions, bases of value, methodology and reporting requirements that a professional valuation should follow.
A Blue Book valuation is one prepared in accordance with those standards by a valuer accredited to apply them. In Ireland, TEGoVA's accreditations — Recognised European Valuer (REV) and TEGoVA Residential Valuer (TRV) — are awarded through IPAV, the only Irish institute with awarding rights.
Blue Book and Red Book
People often ask which standard they need. Both are recognised professional valuation frameworks: the Blue Book is TEGoVA's European Valuation Standards, applied across the EU and administered in Ireland through IPAV; the Red Book is the RICS Valuation Global Standards, applied through RICS and the SCSI.
They cover much the same ground and both produce valuations to a recognised professional standard. What matters in practice is what the party receiving the report requires. If your lender, solicitor or institution has specified one, tell us at the outset. If they have not specified, ask them — and if you would rather not, we are happy to ask on your behalf.
Why it matters
Working to a defined standard is what separates a professional valuation from an estimate. It means the basis of value is stated explicitly, the methodology is recognised, the assumptions are disclosed, and the report contains what a reader needs in order to test the conclusion.
That is the same idea as our tagline, expressed in a formal standard. Evidence before opinion is not just how we prefer to work; it is what EVS requires.

Loan to Value (LTV) Valuation
Loan to Value (LTV) Valuations
If your property is worth more than when you bought it, an up-to-date valuation may support a move into a lower interest rate band. Several of the main lenders have a formal process for exactly this.
What is loan to value?
Loan to value is the size of your mortgage expressed as a percentage of the property's value. Borrow €300,000 against a €400,000 property and your LTV is 75%.
Most Irish lenders price their mortgage rates in LTV bands — typically breaking at 90%, 80%, 70%, 60% and 50%. The lower the band, the lower the risk to the lender, and the lower the rate on offer.
Why this is worth ten minutes of your attention
Your LTV falls over time for two reasons at once: you pay down the balance, and the property changes in value. Neither happens on your lender's initiative. Your rate is set against the LTV recorded when the loan was drawn down, and it generally stays there until somebody evidences a change.
That means a homeowner who bought several years ago may already be in a cheaper band and simply paying the older rate. The only thing standing between them and it is a current valuation showing where the property now sits.
Worked example: a mortgage of €270,000 on a property valued at €300,000 sits at 90% LTV. If the property is now worth €340,000 and the balance has fallen to €255,000, the LTV is 75% — two bands lower. Whether that translates into a lower rate depends on your lender and your product, but where it does, the saving over the remaining term is set against a single one-off valuation fee.
When to get an LTV valuation
Property prices in your area have risen since you bought
You have been paying down the mortgage for several years
You have extended, renovated or materially improved the property
You are switching lender and want to secure the best band available
You are coming off a fixed rate and choosing a new product
You are considering a top-up or equity release and need to know where you stand
Before you instruct anyone — check three things with your lender
An LTV band move is a formal process, and each lender runs it slightly differently. One phone call before you spend anything will tell you whether it is worth doing.
Does the valuer need to be on the lender's approved panel? Several of the main lenders will only accept a report from a valuer on their own list.
How recent must the valuation be? Lenders typically require a report dated within the last four to six months, so timing matters.
Are you on a fixed rate? If so, moving rate before the fixed period ends can trigger a breakage cost. Some lenders let you apply in the months before your fixed rate matures.
